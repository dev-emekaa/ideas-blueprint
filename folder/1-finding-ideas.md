# Finding Ideas

How to find a product idea that already makes money for others, check the evidence behind it, and pick the one worth building.

Do the research yourself with the sections below, or run the **idea research prompt** at the end. It searches the same sources and runs the same checks.

---

## The strategy

- **Build where people already pay.** If people already pay to solve a problem, whether to competitors or for workarounds, demand is proven. What's left to prove is whether you can win a share of it.
- **Win on one clear angle.** Users don't switch for a slightly better product, because switching costs them time and effort. You need one reason that makes switching obvious for a specific group:
  - a narrower audience the leaders serve badly
  - a complaint the leaders ignore
  - a better pricing model
  - a platform, region or language they don't cover
  - a much simpler or faster product
  - a cheaper way to reach the users than the leaders have
- **Strong categories:** products that remove a costly problem (lost money or time), help people quit a habit (porn, sugar, smoking, doomscrolling), or help them reach a goal (fitness, appearance, money, learning). Habit and goal apps can pass $10k a month, but they are crowded, so the angle decides.

## Where to find candidates

### Revenue databases

Start here. They show what is already earning.

- **[TrustMRR](https://trustmrr.com)** — free. Revenue is verified directly through each startup's payment provider (Stripe, RevenueCat, Superwall, Paddle, Lemon Squeezy, Polar and others), so it covers web products and mobile apps. Look for products earning well in a niche that isn't crowded.
- **[ScreensDesign](https://screensdesign.com)** — iOS apps' screens alongside revenue and install figures, filterable by category. It doesn't say where its revenue figures come from, so check them in Sensor Tower or Appfigures.
- **App Store and Google Play top-grossing charts** — free, by category and country. Look below the giants, roughly positions 20 to 200, for apps earning well despite weak ratings or dated design.
- **[Sensor Tower](https://sensortower.com) and [Appfigures](https://appfigures.com)** — revenue and download estimates for any app. Better for checking a candidate than for finding one.

### Founders sharing their numbers

- **X** — indie founders post revenue milestones. Check the numbers before trusting them.
- **TikTok and Instagram** — search for and engage with app-building and startup content so your feed shows more of it. Search "app startup" and industry keywords, and sort by most liked. An app whose videos keep drawing engagement has demand and a content angle that works.
- **Social Growth Engineers** — consumer apps' growth stories and revenue numbers.

### Ads

An ad that has run for 60 days or more, especially in several versions of the same hook, is likely profitable. Running ads alone proves little, because funded companies can advertise at a loss.

- **[Meta Ad Library](https://www.facebook.com/ads/library)** — every active ad on Facebook and Instagram, with the date it started running.
- **[TikTok Creative Center — Top Ads](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/creativecenter)** — high-performing ads, filterable by industry.

### Complaints

**Reddit, niche forums, Hacker News, and competitors' 1–2★ reviews** on the App Store, Google Play, G2, Capterra and Trustpilot. Reviews show exactly where a competitor is weak, which is often your angle.

A complaint counts as a signal only when it is:
- **Recurring:** the same problem in at least 3 independent threads or reviews from the last 12–24 months
- **Costly:** it costs people money or time, or forces manual workarounds
- **Already paid for:** people pay for a poor tool, a workaround or someone to do it by hand. A popular request for a free tool is a weak signal.

### Emerging categories

**[Y Combinator's latest batches](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies)** and its **[Requests for Startups](https://www.ycombinator.com/rfs)** show what investors expect to grow. Funding is not proof of demand, and many batch companies have no revenue yet. Use them to spot categories, then look for revenue proof elsewhere.

## Checking the numbers

**Trust them in this order: verified, then estimated, then claimed.** TrustMRR figures are verified. Sensor Tower and Appfigures figures are estimates. Screenshots and posts are claims.

**Estimates are rough.** They multiply estimated downloads by estimated revenue per user. For smaller apps they can be off by 2× or more, and iOS estimates are weaker than Android ones.
- Use them for order of magnitude: they separate a $10k-a-month app from a $1M-a-month app, not $40k from $60k.
- Check the same app in both tools. If they disagree by 2× or more, treat the number as a wide range.
- Compare apps within the same tool. Ratios are more reliable than absolute figures.

**Check whether a figure is before or after the store's cut.** Apple and Google take 15% (on their small-business rates, for the first $1M a year) to 30%.

**Look for consistency.** Six months or more of steady or growing revenue counts. A single spike does not.

## The scorecard

Fill one in for each candidate.

**Must-pass checks.** If any one fails, drop the idea:

1. **Revenue proof** — money is already spent on the problem: several competitors have earned steadily for six months or more, or people pay for workarounds such as agencies, freelancers or poor tools. One giant and nothing else does not count. Supporting signs: reviews keep arriving, complaints are about missing features or price rather than "it doesn't work", ads run for months, and the products update often.
2. **Your angle** — you can say in one sentence why users would switch to you.
3. **A channel you can reach** — you can name the specific, affordable way you'll reach the primary users: content, search, app-store search, a community, or an audience you already have.

**Scored checks.** Score each 1–5, where 5 is best:

| Check | What to look at |
| --- | --- |
| Demand trend | Revenue and download trend over 6–12 months; [Google Trends](https://trends.google.com) for the problem's keywords |
| Pain and willingness to pay | How strongly people complain, and whether they already pay for poor tools or workarounds |
| Competitor weakness | Ratings, the themes in 1–2★ reviews, time since the last update, pricing users resent |
| Revenue spread | Many mid-size earners (5) versus one winner taking almost everything (1) |
| Unit economics | Your estimated lifetime value against the acquisition cost your channel implies (below) |
| Build effort | Can you ship a first version in six weeks or less? |
| Risk | Platform rules, regulation, dependence on one API, a passing trend (5 = low risk) |
| Founder fit | You understand these users and would stay with this for two years or more |

Compare totals only between ideas that passed all three must-pass checks.

### Unit economics

- **Revenue per paying user:** the monthly price actually paid, after discounts, annual-plan averaging and the store's cut.
- **Monthly churn:** the share of paying users who cancel each month.
- **Gross margin:** what's left after the costs of serving a user, including AI model costs for AI products.
- **Lifetime value (LTV)** = monthly revenue per paying user × gross margin ÷ monthly churn.
  For example, $10 a month at 80% margin with 8% monthly churn gives $10 × 0.8 ÷ 0.08 = **$100**.
- **Customer acquisition cost (CAC):** what it costs to win one paying user. For ads, it is the cost per install divided by the share of installs that pay. For organic content, count your time.
- **Targets:** LTV at least 3× CAC, with CAC paid back within 12 months. Annual plans often pay it back at purchase.

For churn, trial conversion and revenue-per-install benchmarks by category, use RevenueCat's yearly **[State of Subscription Apps](https://www.revenuecat.com/report)** report.

## The idea research prompt

It does the research above for you, in one of two modes: **find ideas** in a niche (or anywhere), or **evaluate an idea** you already have. Either way it reports where each idea came from and the result of every check.

### How to use

1. **Best:** Claude with a browser you're signed into (Claude in Chrome, or the Claude desktop app's built-in browser). It can then open sites that need a login, such as X, TikTok, Instagram and any paid tool you subscribe to.
   **Otherwise:** any AI with web search or a deep-research mode.
2. Copy the prompt and fill in the **Task** section.
3. It asks you once for anything it couldn't open, with the exact lookup to do. Paste the results, or reply "skip".

### The prompt

```markdown
# Role
You are an idea analyst for a founder who builds software products in markets already proven to make money. You find candidates, check them against evidence, and report only what the evidence supports.

# Task
Keep one mode and delete the other.
- Find ideas. Niche or audience: [e.g. "independent landlords", or "open"] · Product type: [B2B, consumer, or either] · Platforms: [web, mobile, or either] · Exclude: [markets to skip, or "none"] · Sources: [all, or the ones to focus on, e.g. "complaints only"]
- Evaluate my idea: [describe it, or name a competitor's product to build a better version of]

# Access
- Use web search for public pages.
- If you can control a browser I'm signed into, use it for sites that need a login. Read only: never post, like, follow, message or change a setting.
- Never guess what a page you couldn't open says. Add it to the "Check yourself" list.

# Sources
1. Verified revenue: TrustMRR (trustmrr.com)
2. App revenue estimates: Sensor Tower, Appfigures, and ScreensDesign for iOS apps
3. Store charts: App Store and Google Play top-grossing by category and country, roughly positions 20 to 200
4. Founders sharing numbers: X, TikTok, Instagram, Social Growth Engineers
5. Ads: Meta Ad Library (with start dates), TikTok Creative Center Top Ads
6. Complaints: Reddit, niche forums, Hacker News, and 1–2★ reviews on the App Store, Google Play, G2, Capterra and Trustpilot
7. Emerging categories: Y Combinator's latest batches and Requests for Startups
To find ideas, search all of them within the focus. To evaluate an idea, use them to find its direct and indirect competitors and to run the checks.

# Evidence rules
- Trust verified revenue over estimates, and estimates over claims. Label which each figure is.
- Estimates for small apps can be off by 2× or more: treat them as order of magnitude, and compare two tools where you can.
- Say whether a revenue figure is before or after the app store's 15–30% cut.
- An ad counts as a signal only if it has run for 60 days or more.
- A complaint counts only if the same problem appears in at least 3 independent threads or reviews, costs people money or time, and people already pay for a poor tool, a workaround or someone to do it by hand. A popular request for a free tool is a weak signal.
- Prefer evidence from the last 12 to 24 months. Link every claim to its source. Never invent figures.

# Checks
Must-pass. An idea that fails any one is rejected:
1. Revenue proof: money is already spent on the problem. Several competitors have earned steadily for 6 months or more, or people pay for workarounds such as agencies, freelancers or poor tools. One giant and nothing else does not count.
2. Angle: one sentence on why users would switch, such as a narrower audience the leaders serve badly, a complaint they ignore, better pricing, a platform, region or language they don't cover, a much simpler product, or a cheaper way to reach users.
3. Channel: a specific, affordable way to reach the primary users, such as content, search, app-store search or a community.

Scored 1 to 5 (5 is best), each with a one-line reason and its source:
- Demand trend: revenue and download trend over 6 to 12 months, and search interest
- Pain and willingness to pay: how strongly people complain, and whether they already pay for poor tools or workarounds
- Competitor weakness: ratings, themes in 1–2★ reviews, time since last update, pricing users resent
- Revenue spread: many mid-size earners (5) versus one winner taking almost everything (1)
- Unit economics: lifetime value (monthly revenue per paying user × gross margin ÷ monthly churn) against the acquisition cost the channel implies; the target is at least 3:1. Use category benchmarks such as RevenueCat's State of Subscription Apps report, labelled as assumptions.
- Build effort: can a first version ship in 6 weeks or less?
- Risk: platform rules, regulation, dependence on one API, a passing trend (5 = low risk)
Leave founder fit to me.

# How you work
1. Gather. Finding ideas: collect candidates from the sources and keep those with signs that people pay. Evaluating: find the direct and indirect competitors.
2. Check. Run the must-pass checks, then score what passes.
3. Ask once. List every lookup you couldn't do under "Check yourself", each with exactly what to look up and why (for example: "Sensor Tower: [app], monthly revenue for the last 6 months, worldwide"). Ask me to paste the results or reply "skip". Then complete the checks; anything skipped stays marked "unverified".
4. Report.

# Report
Finding ideas: the 5 strongest ideas that passed every must-pass check, ranked by total score. Evaluating: one entry.
For each idea:
- **Idea:** the problem, who has it, and the product that solves it, in 2 to 3 sentences
- **Found in:** the sources that surfaced it, linked
- **Competitors and workarounds:** name, price, revenue (verified, estimated or claimed, with source) and main weakness of each
- **Must-pass checks:** pass or fail, with the evidence
- **Scores:** a table of check, score, reason and source
- **Riskiest assumption:** and a one-week test for it
- **Verdict:** build, change (say how), or drop
Then list the rejected candidates, one line each, naming the check they failed.
```
